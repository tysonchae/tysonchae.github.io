---
title: "AI, the New Pair Programmer"
tags: [team, culture, retro, ai]
---

Up until last year, I watched a ton of live coding streams on YouTube. Seeing other developers turn app ideas into reality was incredibly insightful—it let me see how they brainstormed and worked through the build process one step at a time.

Working in the industry gives you plenty of chances to see colleagues think out loud during real-time debugging, but everyday feature work is a different story. You can only piece together so much from the tech stack, tools, and final code—**the actual reasoning behind it usually stays hidden**. Live coding closed that gap for me.

Back in 2017, I was assigned my first microservice project. I partnered with a developer who helped shape the domain model and build out features, and honestly, their coding ability was on another level. Because of some technical limitations at the time, we had to duplicate shared logic across multiple microservices and everyone knew a refactor was coming eventually. Even within those constraints, this developer structured and designed the code beautifully.

Working side by side—sitting at my desk, talking through our thinking as we coded—accelerated my growth enormously. I'd already learned plenty from the usual design-code-review cycle, but **watching someone type out code line by line while explaining their reasoning in real time was a completely different level of learning**. It gave me the chance to compare our approaches and spot weaknesses in my own thinking.

Jump to 2026, and GenAI tools now handle nearly all the coding. I make a conscious effort not to write code by hand anymore. Even when the AI messes up, I just flag the issue and tell it how to fix things instead of typing the fix myself, that way the AI keeps full context and doesn't lose track of the codebase.

Since I barely write code directly anymore, it can feel like live coding and pair programming—practices I once valued so much—have disappeared for good.

**But when I really think about it, the AI has essentially stepped into the role of that peer.**

Lately, during code reviews, I keep running into developers who can't explain what they actually committed or changed, because the AI did all the work. **That's genuinely concerning to me.** It's an even bigger issue with junior developers who don't have strong fundamentals yet. As long as the feature runs, they'll commit it without understanding why it's built that way or what actually changed.

> We need to treat working with AI the same way we treated pair programming. We have to slow down, understand what the AI produced and why, and keep asking questions until it actually makes sense to us.

Sure, developers are writing less code these days but **without truly understanding what the AI generates, there's no real professional growth**.
