---
title: "What Makes a Good Teammate?"
tags: [mentorship, feedback, team-culture, growth]
---

When I worked as a contractor across different sites, I mostly worked on short-term projects. In a relatively short period of time, I got to meet many different people and work in very different environments.

I've also been at my current company for quite a few years now. But every year feels different—the atmosphere changes, managers change, and the people I work with change. So in a way, every year brings a different experience.

Sometimes I find myself wondering:

**When was I happiest at work?**

Looking back, the answer is pretty clear.

It was when **someone genuinely cared about my growth and thought about it together with me.**

Some companies I worked for had really strong mentoring systems. Mentors would closely observe their mentees, think about what they were missing, what they needed to learn, and what direction they could grow in. In some cases, mentoring was even part of the performance review.

On the other hand, if mentoring is simply about assigning someone and saying, "Ask them if you don't know something," it's hard to expect much beyond answering questions.

Because helping someone grow takes time and energy.

And people grow at different speeds and to different depths.

Looking back, the developers I appreciated most weren't necessarily people who avoided giving me hard feedback. They were willing to say difficult things when necessary.

But more importantly, **they could tell me specifically what I was missing and what I needed to do next.**

When I first experienced microservices, I had a mentor who shared his code with me and explained how he thought about problems and approached engineering decisions. He recommended books, suggested things I should study, and helped me understand what I needed at that point in my career.

Another person once told me, "If you want to become a real engineer, look at how this person works," and gave me a concrete example to learn from.

Someone else helped me understand the difference between things I could achieve through effort and things that were much harder to develop simply through hard work.

Some of those conversations were painful.

But at the same time, they helped me recognize other strengths I had and figure out how to build on them.

Looking back, the best mentors weren't necessarily the people who gave me the right answers.

They were the people who **helped me see myself more clearly.**

---

And that leads me to another question.

**What should we do when someone on the team is struggling or isn't performing well yet?**

Some people might simply become frustrated with them and end up saying negative things behind their backs.

Others might choose to stay neutral because they don't want to get involved in politics, or because they simply want to be a nice person.

They don't say anything positive.

But they don't say anything negative either.

But is that really helping the person?

Is it really only the manager's responsibility to help someone grow?

Does the manager know everything?

And even if they do, can they always say everything that needs to be said?

Maybe we've made it too easy to treat someone else's growth as solely a manager's responsibility.

There are teammates who work closely with that person. They see their code, how they solve problems, how they communicate, and how they approach their work.

So do teammates really have no responsibility at all?

---

Which brings me back to the original question:

**What makes a good teammate?**

Is it someone who is kind to me?

Someone who is easy to work with?

Someone I rarely have conflicts with?

Of course, those things matter.

But maybe a truly trustworthy teammate is someone who can tell me **what I need to hear, rather than simply what I want to hear.**

Someone who can give me difficult feedback because they genuinely want me to become better—not because they are judging me.

When I think about the best developers I've worked with, they weren't simply people who evaluated me.

They showed me things I couldn't see for myself.

They pointed out what I was missing.

They helped me recognize strengths I didn't know I had.

**They thought with me about who I could become.**

So these days, when I think about what makes someone a good teammate, I find myself asking:

> **"How honest can this person be with me if that honesty helps me become better?"**

Maybe that's what being trustworthy really means.
