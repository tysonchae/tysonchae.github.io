---
title: "What Decision Does This Person Need to Make Right Now?"
tags: [communication, leadership, retro]
---

I want to write down a mistake I made a few days ago.

Our team was using a test database, and one day the refresh suddenly started failing. Normally, QA could run it without any problems.

We asked the DBA team for help, but they couldn't figure out the cause. After trying a few times, the DBA eventually gave up. We were told to wait until another DBA, who had originally written the script, came back.

Then my manager came over to the QA team and asked:

> “The refresh isn’t working?”

I knew the history of the database, so I immediately started explaining.

When the problem started, what had happened since then, what the DBA had tried, and so on.

After a few sentences, I looked at my manager’s face.

Ah.

That wasn’t what he was asking.

I stopped talking.

My manager wasn’t asking me to explain what had happened.

He wanted to know what he needed to do now.

I should have answered something like:

> “Please escalate this to the infrastructure team or DBA management. The current DBA couldn’t identify the root cause, and we don’t know when the person who wrote the script will be back. Project A depends on this database, and if this isn’t resolved today, the project schedule will be blocked.”

In the end, my manager escalated the issue. The script was fixed, and the refresh started working again.

But I kept thinking about this interaction for the rest of the day.

At first, I thought the lesson was simply that I needed to give my manager more accurate information.

But the more I thought about it, the more I realized that wasn’t the real problem.

My manager wasn’t asking for the history of the problem.

**He was trying to understand what was blocking the project and what action he needed to take.**

And I think that distinction matters a lot.

When an explanation gets too long, even accurate information stops being useful. The other person starts wondering:

> “What are you trying to tell me?”

Especially when you’re talking to someone who needs to make a decision, I think the better approach is to tell them the action they need to take first, and then give them a short, strong reason why.

Not:

> “Here’s everything that happened…”

But:

> “Here’s what I think you need to do. Here’s why.”

Or, conclusion first, then what happened.

Tell them what they need to do first. Then give them the reason.

That’s the communication habit I want to build.
